01Executive Summary

Market research has always traded depth for scale. Sagan refuses the trade: it's an AI interviewer — it speaks, listens, probes, and follows up, holding a real research conversation with physicians and patients at whatever scale a study needs. ZoomRx has been AI-first from the start; Sagan is that conviction applied to the interview itself.

I designed the interview experience across web and mobile: the conversation and its turn-taking, the captioning system, the switches between spoken and on-screen answers, and the recovery paths for an audience that gets interrupted for a living.

Submission rates and participation increased by ~30% versus text-based formats — and in revenue, Sagan grew from $0 to $2 million in 2026.

02Problem & Opportunity

Market research offers two bad options. A qualitative interview delivers depth — but needs a trained human moderator, a scheduled call, and budgets that keep samples small. A survey delivers scale — but flattens open answers into typed fragments from respondents racing to finish. The depth-versus-scale tradeoff is structural, and physician time makes it worse: the people whose answers matter most have the least patience for either format.

Because ZoomRx designs the studies itself — the drafts, the paths, the logic — an AI could credibly hold the moderator's chair: the conversation probes freely inside a structure we already know.

Design challenge

How might we make an interview with an AI feel like talking to a good human moderator — for a professional audience with zero patience for friction?

03Insights That Shaped the Product

A conversation needs a visible turn-taker

When someone speaks to a machine, the twin anxieties are "did it hear me?" and "should I be talking now?" Every state — listening, processing, moderator speaking — had to be explicit, or respondents talk over the AI, repeat themselves, and trust collapses.

The screen remembers what sound can't

Spoken words disappear the moment they're said. So the screen keeps the record: captions while the AI talks, and a history of every answer already given. If you missed something or want to check something, it's there.

Some questions need to be seen, not heard

Talking is the richest way to answer "why." But when a question involves something to look at — an ad, a reference image, a set of options to compare — voice alone fails: you can't speak a picture. Those moments move to the screen, and the design had to know which was which, question by question.

04Key Product Decisions

Decision 1 — An interviewer, not a form with a voice

What we didn't do: an open-ended chatbot that wanders wherever the respondent takes it — impressive in a demo, indefensible in a recorded research conversation.

Sagan doesn't read a questionnaire aloud — it runs the interview. It introduces itself, asks, listens, and probes with follow-ups the way a good moderator digs into an interesting answer. We could let it do that safely because we design the studies ourselves: the AI probes freely inside a structure whose every path we already know.

Tradeoff: an AI that probes can drift off-study. Bounding it to the research structure gave up some spontaneity — and bought the reliability a recorded medical-adjacent conversation cannot do without.

Decision 2 — Whose turn it is, always visible

A voice conversation fails the moment nobody knows whose turn it is. Every state is explicit — Preparing your interview room. Connecting your moderator. Setting up audio. Listening… Processing your response… — with a persistent indicator showing whether the moderator or you holds the floor. Even the pre-interview sequence is deliberate ritual: it frames what follows as a meeting, not a form.

Tradeoff: more visible machinery than a minimal aesthetic would want. Accepted — in voice, reassurance beats minimalism.

Decision 3 — Captions, capped at eight words

What we didn't do: caption the respondent's own speech. Watching your words appear and get interpreted in real time pulls your attention onto the transcription instead of your answer — distracting at best, disorienting when the interpretation lags or misses. Captions run one way only: the AI's side of the conversation.

Everything Sagan says is captioned on screen — and each caption holds a maximum of eight words, so the text stays big enough to read at a glance. Nothing gets missed, with or without sound.

Tradeoff: the cap constrains the writing itself — every question has to be phrased for the ear and for an eight-word screen. Harder to write, easier to follow.

Decision 4 — Voice where it's rich, screen where it's precise

Open-ended answers are spoken. But a 1–6 rating is a tap, a stimulus is an image on screen, and a multi-select is a checklist with typed input where the options don't fit. The conversation switches modality to whatever makes the answer most accurate.

Tradeoff: switching between speaking and tapping mid-conversation risks breaking the flow — the turn-taking states exist partly to make those hand-offs feel natural.

Decision 5 — The interview survives real life

Clinicians get interrupted. Sagan notices silence ("Are you there?"), offers breaks, saves progress — and when the respondent returns, it recaps what was already discussed before continuing. The interview ends properly too: completion, the honorarium payment initiated, and a transcript-style history the respondent can revisit.

Tradeoff: an interruptible interview is more work to design than a linear one. But an interview that punishes interruption would select against exactly the busy, senior respondents the research wants most.

05Experience Design

The interview, end to end

Profile verification → the study card with its honorarium → guidance (find a quiet spot; don't share personally identifying information) → the room-preparation sequence → the conversation → completion, payment initiated, dashboard. One arc, identical across web and mobile — the interview is the product on both.

The history state

A transcript-style view lets respondents recall every answer they've given. The conversation is never a black box, and stepping away never means losing your place.

06Validation & Iteration

Two problems were the hardest, and both came from the same place: in an AI interview, the user gets lost easily.

The first: whose turn is it? In early versions, respondents couldn't tell when the AI was speaking, when it was their turn — or why there was silence while the AI prepared a follow-up. Every one of those gaps broke the conversation: people talked over the moderator, repeated themselves, or sat waiting for nothing. Defining the full set of states — moderator speaking, listening, processing, preparing the next question — was the most complicated design work in the product, precisely because human conversation makes turn-taking invisible and an AI conversation has to make it explicit.

The second: how much should the screen say while the AI speaks?

  • We started audio-only — the purest version of a conversation. It had a real flaw: if a respondent misses something the AI said, there's no way to recover it — and in an interview, a missed question is a broken answer.
  • So we added closed captions. But a wall of text pulls attention away from listening and quietly turns the conversation back into reading.
  • The answer was YouTube-style captioning: short lines, a maximum of eight words at a time — big, glanceable, gone. Enough to catch what you missed, never enough to compete with the voice.
  • And for anything beyond the moment, the history state — recall any answer already given, so nothing depends on memory.

That sequence — audio-only → captions → capped captions + history — is the product's learning, in shipped form. And the two threads together — the turn-taking states and the caption system — are why the interview feels effortless: the hardest work in the product is the machinery you barely notice.

07Impact & Learnings

What changed in practice

Submission rates and participation increased by ~30% versus text-based formats — the format, not respondent willingness, was the constraint. $0 → $2 million in revenue in 2026 — the commercial answer arrived fast.

It also outgrew its starting point. Sagan has since grown into Sagan Agents — ZoomRx's agentic operating system for pharma insights, spanning archive intelligence, agentic market research, and strategic insights agents — and the public page carries real HCP feedback describing the interviews as concise, straightforward, and well-directed. The interview experience I designed is where that platform began.

Honest limits on what I can claim

ZoomRx work: the 30% figure is the shareable headline; underlying data and client specifics remain internal.

Learnings I've taken forward

Changing how people answer — speaking instead of typing — moved the results more than years of refining what they were asked. The input method turned out to be the biggest design lever in the product, bigger than any screen-level improvement.

The principles Sagan Agents now markets as human-in-the-loop — a process you can see, a conversation you can intervene in, answers you can trace — were in the interview's DNA from the start: visible turn-taking, bounded probing, a history you can always check. Designing trust into the first product turned out to be designing the philosophy of the platform.

01Executive Summary

Gribb's platform starts with a piece of hardware: a Geotab telematics device installed in delivery vehicles. It reports, live, how each vehicle is being driven — harsh braking, sudden acceleration, hard turns, idling, and more. For the delivery companies running these fleets — DHL and UPS among them — that stream holds the answers to expensive questions. Raw, it's unreadable.

As Lead Designer over two years, I designed both sides of that translation: the dashboard that fleet clients, insurers, and resellers used to understand every vehicle, driver, and trip — and the driver app that turned safe driving into a score worth competing for, with real rewards behind it.

The honest scope of this product is large: multi-tenant client management, a configurable rule engine, fleet analytics, fuel economics, exception maps, shift management, damage reporting, driver gamification. The design job was to make sure no single user ever felt that size:

  • A driver sees a score and a game
  • A manager sees their fleet and what needs attention
  • An insurer sees risk and evidence
The complexity lives in the system; the simplicity lives in each person's slice of it — keeping that separation was the work.

02Problem & Opportunity

A fleet operator cares about a small number of questions: who is driving dangerously, which vehicles need attention, and where the operation is losing money. The Geotab device answers all of them — but in the form of thousands of raw data points per vehicle per day. No operations manager at a delivery company reads telemetry, and no driver changes how they brake because a sensor logged an event.

The gap between what the device knew and what a client could act on — that gap was the product.

Design challenge

How might we turn one live telemetry stream into something two very different audiences act on — managers making decisions about their fleet, and drivers making decisions behind the wheel?

Told from both seats — the manager's and the driver's. Same telemetry, two working lives.

03Insights That Shaped the Product

Managers and drivers need the same data for opposite reasons

A fleet manager looks at driving behaviour to hold the operation accountable. A driver looks at it to know how they're doing. One audience needs oversight; the other needs motivation. Designing one interface for both would have served neither — the data had to wear two faces.

Events matter more than streams

Nobody watches telemetry. People respond to things that happen — a collision, a harsh-braking spike, a vehicle trending toward a fault. The dashboard had to surface events and exceptions instantly and let ordinary operation stay quiet.

A score only changes driving if drivers accept it

A driving score changes conversations between managers and drivers — and drivers push back on numbers they can't trace. The score had to be legible (built from named events like harsh braking and idling, through rules anyone could understand) before it could be motivating. Gamification only works on top of a score people believe.

04Key Product Decisions

The two hardest problems of the project sat next to each other: defining the rule engine — deciding how driving would be judged at all — and designing a dashboard that could hold this much product without collapsing. The gamification system and the plate-scan check-in were hard in a different way: they had to make sophisticated machinery feel effortless to the person using it.

Decision 1 — One data model, two faces

The client dashboard and the driver app were designed as one system serving opposite needs. The dashboard answers the manager's questions: who is driving dangerously, which vehicles need attention, what does the fleet look like tonight. The app answers the driver's: how am I doing, where do I stand, what's my score. Same telemetry, same rule engine, opposite framing.

Tradeoff: one coherent system is slower to design upfront than two independent products. It paid back constantly — every metric added to the rule engine appeared on both sides without rework, and the score a manager saw always matched the score a driver was chasing.

Decision 2 — Configuration over customisation: the rule engine

The manager's seat.
What we didn't do: hard-code one universal definition of bad driving — the same thresholds for every client, vehicle, and weather condition. Far simpler to build, and wrong everywhere except the average case.

We didn't hard-code what "bad driving" means — because it doesn't mean one thing. Behaviour events flowed through a rule engine where every rule carries context: the same harsh braking scores differently on a snowy night in a truck than on a dry highway in a van. Road type, vehicle category, day versus night, even weather — all weighted, all configurable per client, down to the score bands and the colours that represent them.

Tradeoff: building the engine before the interface cost time when nothing visual existed to show for it, and every configurable option is a screen an admin has to understand. But it meant one product could serve every client's definition of safe driving without redesign — and the score stayed explainable, because every point traced back to a named rule.

Decision 3 — Due process for the algorithm

A score that judges people needs a way to be wrong. If a driver believed an exception was unfair — the speed limit was different, the idling had a reason — they could raise a query from the app, state their case, and have it land with their manager, who reviews the trip and marks the query valid or invalid. Valid queries correct the record.

Tradeoff: an appeals process invites appeals — it costs manager time and admits the system makes mistakes. We took that cost deliberately: a contestable score gets trusted and a black box gets gamed. The dispute flow is what let the same number carry a manager's judgement and a driver's pay-adjacent rewards without being resented.

Decision 4 — Gamification with real stakes

The driver's seat.
What we didn't do: ship the reporting-only version — telemetry flowing one way, upward to managers. It was the obvious MVP, and it would have made the app the driver's enemy on day one.

Surveillance data has a dignity problem: a system that only reports drivers to their managers gets resented and gamed. So the driver app flipped the framing. The score became a progression system — levels, streaks, milestones — with leaderboards ranked against people drivers actually know ("best 5% in your hub", not an anonymous global list), 30-second improvement lessons that themselves earn points, and coins that redeem into real Amazon gift cards.

Tradeoff: paying for scores invites chasing scores. The counterweight was the rule engine's context-awareness and the dispute flow — a score that's fair and explainable is hard to cheat and worth winning.

05Experience Design

The shift is the spine

The driver's seat.

On the driver's side, everything hangs off one unit: the shift. Check-in by scanning the vehicle's number plate — the camera does the work, because a driver starting a route shouldn't be fighting a form. The check-in binds driver to vehicle, which is what makes per-driver scoring possible at all on shared fleets. Then the pre-vehicle safety check, the scored trips, any damage reports (tap the vehicle diagram where the damage is, add photos), and check-out. Each shift closes with its own score, its own condition record, its own paper trail — one driver, one vehicle, one accountable session.

The dashboard reads outward from the same spine

The manager's seat.

Fleet overview → vehicles → drivers → trips → exceptions, each level opening into the one below, down to a single braking event on a map.   • Problems find the manager, not the other way around. Open the dashboard and anything wrong is already in front of you; serious incidents interrupt with an alert, and everything running normally stays quietly in the background.

The same two rules hold on the driver's side: the score is always one glance away, and tapping it always shows you why it is what it is.

  • Driver queries — every disputed exception told us exactly where the scoring felt wrong to the people being scored. The people most affected by the system were also its most honest testers.

So when a score looked wrong or slow, we could see exactly which step broke instead of guessing.

  • Fixing onboarding

02Problem & Opportunity

A survey platform has an unusual property: its users are also its supply chain. If physicians dislike the experience, they respond less, answer worse, and eventually leave the panel — and the company's product (research data) degrades with them. NPS at −8 wasn't a vanity-metric problem; it was a leading indicator of supply risk.

The experience problems were structural. Surveys were long and cognitively expensive — dense grids, repetitive patterns, unclear progress. There was no systematic way to hear what respondents thought at the moment they finished. And with over a thousand live surveys authored across teams, quality varied wildly — the experience depended on who had built the survey, not on a system.

Design challenge

How might we make surveys that busy physicians don't dread — at a scale where no individual screen can be hand-polished?

03Insights That Shaped the Product

The feedback had to come from the moment, not the memory

Asking respondents later what they thought produces politeness. Asking at the instant they finish a survey — while the friction is fresh — produces the truth. The End of Survey Feedback (EOSF) mechanism became the engine of the whole programme: 30,000+ responses that turned redesign priorities from opinion into evidence.

Cognitive load is the real completion tax

Drop-off didn't correlate with survey length as much as with density — the grids, the repeated matrices, the screens that demanded re-reading. Reducing effort-per-question mattered more than reducing questions.

At 1,000+ surveys, the system is the design

No team can hand-polish a thousand live instruments. Past a certain scale, the only way to raise experience quality is to raise the floor — tokens, patterns, and question templates that make the good version the default version.

04Key Product Decisions

Decision 1 — Build the feedback loop before the redesign

What we didn't do: redesign first and measure later — the instinct every team has when a number is bad.

The EOSF programme came first, deliberately. Redesigning on intuition risked optimising the wrong friction; 30,000+ responses meant every subsequent change had evidence behind it and a measurement after it.

Tradeoff: slower visible progress at the start — a quarter spent instrumenting instead of shipping screens. It converted the rest of the programme from guesswork to compounding.

Decision 2 — Attack cognitive load, not survey length

The redesign targeted effort-per-question: simplified interaction patterns, clearer progress, breaking dense grids into sequenced steps.

Tradeoff: sequencing dense questions adds screens and can lengthen perceived duration — the bet was that lighter screens beat fewer screens. The NPS trajectory and the 24% increase in Patient Load completion rates backed it.

Decision 3 — A design system sized for survey authors, not designers

The system's real users were the people building surveys at volume. It had to make the right pattern the easy pattern — templates and components that produce consistent experience without a designer in the loop. The system's rules are written doctrine, starting with its first principle: the physician is the hero; ZoomRx is the tool. Its discipline runs to the pixel — at one point, four visually indistinguishable navy variants that had drifted into production were consolidated into a single brand navy, on the record, with a date.

The Figma design system contains 200+ components, design tokens and WCAG 2.1 AA patterns for 1,000+ surveys across web and mobile. Its token architecture links primitive values to semantic roles and components; the source inventory also includes 439 icons and 249 illustrations. I packaged the system as Claude and Codex skills and plugins for agentic AI coding.

Tradeoff: systematising removes flexibility, and some studies genuinely need bespoke instruments. The system had to leave escape hatches without letting the escape hatch become the norm.

Onboarding was redesigned using Jobs-to-be-Done and Amplitude funnel/cohort analysis. Monthly active users grew from ~400 to ~550 (+38%), and 90-day retention improved by ~19%. Tradeoff: verification gates add friction exactly where drop-off hurts most — at the door. But a research panel is only worth what its members' identities are worth: the friction buys the integrity the entire product is sold on.

Decision 5 — Never dead-end a willing respondent

What we didn't do: end the experience at a thank-you page.

The survey loop is designed by scenario, and every exit leads somewhere: finish a survey — see your earnings and the next study ("Earn $75 more"); get screened out — land on an apology that immediately offers alternatives; head home — find the next opportunity waiting. A respondent with time and willingness is the scarcest asset the platform has; the design refuses to waste one.

Tradeoff: chaining opportunities risks feeling like a content treadmill. Pay, time estimates, and "what you'll do" sit on every tile so each next step is an informed choice, not an infinite scroll.

05Experience Design

The panelist's world

The dashboard is a working professional's earning surface, and it reads like one: survey tiles carry pay, time estimate, and "what you'll do" up front — respect for the respondent's decision, tile by tile — with early-access badges, guaranteed-pay variants, and honest unavailable states ("available again on…"). Design challenge

How should an AI answer a question when the person asking is paid to be right — and no design conventions exist yet?

03Insights That Shaped the Product

Expert users don't want answers; they want defensible answers

An analyst quotes Ferma's output in reports read by clients. Every response needed a visible chain back to evidence — the interface had to support verification as a first-class behaviour, not a footnote.

Asking is easy in chat; research isn't

A chat flow answers one question at a time. Real research is non-linear — analysts follow several paths, abandon some, come back to old insights and connect them. Constrained to chat alone, they were manually copying pieces of answers into documents to use later. The product had to become a place where research accumulates, not just a place where questions get answered.

The question itself is a design problem

An empty search bar is intimidating when the question isn't framed yet — and a badly scoped question returns three thousand results nobody can use. The design had to help on both ends: suggesting what to ask before typing, and helping narrow the ask when it came back too big.

04Key Product Decisions

No patterns existed to borrow — so this section reads the way it happened: a log of first-principles calls, each recorded on the boards as it was made.

Call 1 — The answer is a designed object, not generated text

What we didn't do: let the model stream free-form prose. Raw generated text — the default every chatbot shipped with — is unscannable and unverifiable for clinical content.

Instead, every answer type got a designed anatomy. Structured summaries with named fields (Efficacy, Safety, Drug, Indications) where the ask was specific; narrative layouts with a strict typographic hierarchy where it was open-ended; long answers split into short points with a background summary up top and drug names bolded; multi-result answers as collapsed cards — with the truncation call recorded on the board at the moment it was made: show the collapsed view, one field visible.

Tradeoff: every format is a template, and a template can mis-fit an answer. We carried formats per ask-type plus a narrative fallback — more design surface to maintain, in exchange for answers an analyst can scan in seconds and defend in a meeting.

Call 2 — Evidence lives inside the answer

Sources are part of the response, not an appendix: citation chips on every claim-carrying block, and a sources panel — fifty references deep when it had to be — with search built in. We explored the panel's form deliberately: paginated at five, at ten, scrolling, scrolling-plus-pagination, laid side by side and compared before choosing. The design bet underneath it all: for this audience, verifiability is usability.

Tradeoff: denser responses and slower reading than a clean chat bubble. Accepted — the audience is paid to verify.

Call 3 — When the question is too big or too vague, the AI helps shape it

What we didn't do: dump three thousand results and call it an answer.

Broad questions got a designed response of their own: the AI explains the scope problem and offers tailored ways to narrow — by category, time period, specific study, demographic, outcome — written as help, with concrete example questions. And before any question is typed, the empty state suggests what's worth asking, because a blank box is a wall when the question isn't framed yet.

Tradeoff: declining to answer risks feeling unhelpful. The nudges had to read as "here's how to get what you actually want" — a copywriting and tone problem as much as an interface one, with response templates written and rewritten for exactly that.

Call 4 — Comparison as a first-class operation

Analysts compare things for a living — trials, drugs, readouts. So comparison isn't a paragraph; it's a table: named rows (Phase, Enrollment, Arms, Efficacy, Safety, Conclusion), two entities side by side, sources attached. And the table earned its own iteration from real feedback: expanding truncated cells one by one was too many clicks, so we added Expand Content to open them all at once — and renamed the old Expand to Fullscreen so the two couldn't be confused.

Tradeoff: tables inside a conversation break the chat metaphor. Accepted without hesitation — the metaphor serves the analyst, not the other way round.

Call 5 — Beyond the chat: research as the container

What we didn't do: keep bolting features onto the chat.

As the product matured, I wrote the argument that the chat paradigm itself was the ceiling — a document proposing what Ferma should become: workflows and tasks as the core concept, with agent selection removed as a user burden (an orchestrator routes to agents invisibly); profiles — business development, competitive intelligence, scientific communications, new product planning — each carrying pre-built key tasks; and spaces where highlighted responses, insights, and charts accumulate per goal, get compared and tracked, and turn into shareable reports. Chat remains how you ask; the workspace is where research lives.

Tradeoff: proposing a structural change is slower and riskier than shipping the next feature — it has to convince people before it can exist. That's why it was written down, with the flows designed alongside the argument.

02Problem & Opportunity

For a Bentley buyer specifying a Mulliner build, the configuration moment is the most important decision of the purchase. Spec choices shift the price by significant amounts, and once committed, materials enter a production pipeline that takes months to reverse. The cost of a wrong call is high — both for the customer and for Bentley.

The original app was built to communicate craftsmanship, not to support that decision. It showed renderings of completed cars and reference imagery of trim options, but customers had no way to see their own combination. Reaching confidence required either imagination — picturing a colour from a swatch and a stitching pattern from a flat image and combining them mentally — or a multi-stop dealer conversation that often stretched across weeks.

What was broken

Dealerships were never under-equipped — physical swatches, material samples, and reference vehicles were all on the floor. What no showroom could offer was the customer's own combination, assembled: this colour on this body with this interior and this stitching. That composed view lived only in the customer's imagination, and confidence built slowly because of it. The digital experience, meanwhile, showed finished reference cars — it communicated the craft without supporting the decision in progress.

Design challenge

How might we let a customer specify a bespoke Bentley in real time, with enough visual confidence to commit, while staying within the performance ceiling of an iPad in a showroom?

03Insights That Shaped the Product

Three findings came out of stakeholder conversations, workflow reviews with Mulliner-trained sales advisors, and internal usability walkthroughs. Each one mapped to a product direction.

Customers don't know what they want until they see it

A Mulliner spec involves combinations of finishes most people have never seen together. Showing a swatch and a description is not the same as showing the car. The product had to render every choice immediately and unconditionally — no apply button, no preview state, no waiting.

Letting customers roam freely didn't help them

The first prototype let users jump between any option in any order. In review, this didn't help anyone: customers got lost, and advisors had to step in to guide. Constraining the experience into a sequence (model → exterior → interior → wheels → personalisation → review) was one of the more important calls in the project, and not the obvious one.

The real output is the build summary, not the screen

What sales advisors valued most wasn't the configurator itself — it was what came out of it: a clean build summary they could hand to the dealership and production team without any translation or rework. The screens exist to produce that document. Once we understood this, sharing stopped being an export feature buried in a menu and became a core surface of the product.

04Key Product Decisions

This is the section where the product took shape. Four decisions defined the experience.

Decision 1 — Real-time visual feedback without 3D

The original brief assumed a 3D rendering pipeline. After evaluating options, the team concluded that real-time 3D was not viable at the visual fidelity Bentley required. Load times would have broken the experience; reducing fidelity would have broken the brand.

What we didn't do: real-time 3D. Not because it was hard — because it couldn't hit Bentley-grade fidelity on showroom iPads, and Bentley does not accept visible quality loss.

Instead of dropping fidelity, we changed the technique:

  • Every customisable component — body, wheels, interior panels, stitching, trim inlays — rendered as a flat PNG layer at a fixed dimension, with pixel-accurate X/Y positioning derived from a master Photoshop layout
  • Colour works through layering, not asset swaps: the body's detail layer — all the lighting, reflections, and shading — sits at roughly 0.8 opacity over a flat colour fill, so any paint colour shows through while the surface detail stays intact
  • One set of body assets wears every colour in the range

The result felt indistinguishable from a render, loaded immediately, and held up on older iPads.

Tradeoff: the asset production pipeline was heavier than 3D would have required. Each new model meant a new layered Photoshop file, with every option matted out at a consistent vanishing point — across the project this came to more than 1,500 PNG assets, produced and positioned by hand. We absorbed that cost in the design team because it was a one-time investment per model and a permanent gain in runtime quality.

Decision 2 — A guided sequence, not free exploration

Free-form configuration was the obvious default, and the one we built first. Usability sessions made the case for replacing it. Customers who could touch anything spent more time wandering than committing; the lack of structure pushed advisors into a coaching role they weren't in the room to perform.

The guided flow (model → exterior → interior → wheels → personalisation → review) gave the experience a rhythm and let advisors step in at predictable moments. We kept the ability to step back through earlier decisions, but removed the ability to skip ahead. The customer always saw the next decision and never lost their place.

Decision 3 — Share as a first-class action

Sharing isn't a flourish at the end of the flow; it's the point. The Review screen is where the configurator earns its keep. A finished build generates a structured summary that goes to the dealer as a single tap, in a format the build team can consume directly.

This decision changed the information architecture. Sharing isn't tucked under a menu; it's the primary action of the final screen, and it's why the product exists.

Decision 4 — Builds persist across sessions

A Mulliner sale spans multiple visits. Builds had to survive between sessions, on the same device or another one. We added a lightweight account model and a build history that an advisor could pull up at the start of the next meeting, with full version state intact.

Validation ran in two channels: moderated usability sessions with Mulliner-trained sales advisors and product stakeholders, and side-by-side comparisons of interaction variants built within the team.

The most informative result of testing wasn't a metric — it was a change in how people behaved. In the free-exploration version of the prototype, advisors kept interrupting the customer to guide them through options. In the guided version, the same advisors stopped narrating and started watching. That shift, seen across multiple sessions, was the strongest argument we had for replacing open navigation.

Other iterations from testing:

  • The hardest interface problem was fitting the customisation controls onto a compact mobile screen. The option selectors ran edge to edge, and on a small display the early versions felt cramped and fiddly to operate accessibly. We stripped out buttons and small controls wherever possible and rebuilt the interactions around tap and scroll — the two gestures nobody gets wrong. That's why the shipped product has so few visible controls.
  • Navigation hierarchy was simplified after early sessions showed customers backing out of nested screens.
  • Visual hierarchy on the review screen was rebuilt to make the share action unmistakable; it had originally been buried as a secondary button.
  • Aligning the layered PNG assets was unforgiving work. Every layer had to sit in exactly the right position — a deviation of even one pixel put a wheel or a trim line visibly out of place and broke the illusion of a single render. Getting each asset set to register perfectly took repeated adjustment rounds between design and engineering.

07Impact & Learnings

What changed in practice

The configurator became the default surface for in-showroom Mulliner conversations. The iPad sat between customer and advisor as the shared surface for composing the complete build — working alongside the physical samples on the floor, not replacing them.

Submissions to the build team became uniform. Every build left the room as the same structured summary — the same option codes, the same ordering — which meant less verification work for the production team downstream.

Customers left sessions with a build they had specified themselves rather than a build the advisor had narrated to them. The qualitative shift that mattered most was the decline in requests to physically see a reference car before committing — the configurator was the reference.

Bentley later pulled the app from public availability, for a practical reason: open access meant anyone could send a build request to a dealership, and many of those requests were casual — sent on a whim, with no response when dealers followed up. Restricting the app to executives and showroom use put it back in the hands of serious buyers, in the room where builds are actually specified. It had a second benefit: on showroom iPads, the compact-mobile-screen problem stopped mattering — the interface had the space it worked best at. The move cut off any public-facing metrics, but the product was retained exactly where it earned its keep.

Honest limits on what I can claim

This was an agency engagement, which means I don't have access to post-launch telemetry from Bentley's side. I can speak to the qualitative shifts I observed in usability sessions, dealer feedback, and stakeholder reviews; I can't claim a specific completion-rate lift or transaction-time reduction. The numbers I would want — sessions per build, share-to-pricing time, build-to-order conversion — sit on Bentley's side and weren't part of the contractual scope.

A portfolio piece is more useful when it's honest about this than when it borrows credibility from numbers nobody can verify.

Learnings I've taken forward

Real-time visual feedback is the most important single factor in a high-value configurator. Every other usability improvement I made on this project, combined, was smaller than the gain from removing the gap between choose and see.

A performance constraint can become a design technique. The layered-PNG system started as a workaround for an absent 3D pipeline; it ended as a faster, sharper rendering approach than 3D would have given us at this fidelity.

The most valuable part of a luxury digital product is what isn't on screen. Everything we removed — the free-form navigation, the apply button, the swatch-only state, the menu-buried share — contributed more to the experience than anything we added.

This case study is deliberately the shortest — the product's promise is saved minutes, and the writing keeps it.

01Executive Summary

Clinicians are obligated to stay current and structurally denied the time to do it. Medical news is scattered across dozens of sources, written long, and rarely filtered for what this specialist actually needs. The staying-current happens or it doesn't — and mostly it doesn't.

Curie is ZoomRx's answer: an AI-powered platform at curie.md that turns the day's healthcare news into multi-source briefs a clinician can clear in minutes — with deep-dive articles where a story deserves them, podcasts (including conference recaps), quick medical word games, and a weekly newsletter around them. Personalised to specialty and, one level deeper, to indication. I designed the product experience end to end: the home and brief formats, the article page, community and identity, notifications, and the mobile experience.

The product's promise is printed in its own header: "min saved today." Every design decision on this page serves the same scarce resource — a clinician's stolen minutes.

02Problem & Opportunity

The constraint on staying current isn't motivation — it's time and relevance. A clinical day offers minutes, not sessions. And generic medical news fails the relevance test, because an oncologist doesn't have time for cardiology headlines either. Relevance had to be built in, per clinician — and the reading had to fit the minutes the day actually gives.

Design challenge

How do you keep a clinician current in the minutes their day actually gives them?

03Insights That Shaped the Product

Minutes are the currency

Every surface bets on the same thing: time given back. Briefs compress multiple sources into a scan; the header counts the minutes saved; even the games pitch themselves honestly — "in between patients… a few minutes, and you're back in the zone."

Specialty isn't personal enough

An oncologist and a dermatologist need different news — but so do two oncologists working different indications. Personalisation goes two levels deep: specialty, then indication, with stories and topics followable one by one.

Trust needs receipts — and real faces

A summarised brief asks to be believed, so every brief carries its sources, one tap away. And a clinician community is only credible if the people in it are real — which is why identity gets verified, not just registered.

04Key Product Decisions

Decision 1 — A brief is built from many sources, not one headline

What we didn't do: aggregate links, like every news app before it. A link list saves nobody any time.

Each brief is an AI-composed summary across sources — seven where seven exist — with an "Inside:" hook and a dive-in article when the story warrants depth: a summary box up top, a "Why it matters" section tabbed by audience (clinician, regulatory expert, researcher, patient-engagement — each reads their own stake), key metrics shown one at a time in tabs to keep density down, real quotes, a regulatory timeline, and a sources button that reveals every reference in place — verify without losing your reading flow.

Tradeoff: summarising the news means owning the summary. The design answer is transparency: sources always one tap away, never buried.

Decision 2 — Cards that know how much time you have

Content comes typed by time-budget: Big Picture for the day's one story, Catch-up for "missed a week?", Deep-Dive, Podcast, Game — a clinician picks by the minutes they have, not by scrolling luck. The home was explored twice around this, with the reasoning written on the boards: a stacked-tiles version for variety and discoverability, and a single-tile, Instagram-style swiping version — one story at a time, lighter sessions — taken all the way to a working prototype in the iOS browser.

Tradeoff: archetypes are an editorial promise — every card type has to be filled, every day.

Decision 3 — Follow the story, not just the feed

News is ongoing, so a developing story can be followed as a story — one tap, updates as it develops — and every article's related topics are followable individually. Personalisation becomes something the reader assembles tap by tap, not a settings page they'll never visit.

Tradeoff: granular following splinters the content per reader — the feed still has to feel full for a clinician following three narrow topics.

Decision 4 — Anonymous first, verified when it matters

What we didn't do: a registration wall.

Everything reads logged-out. Sign-up is asked only at moments of demonstrated value — the third topic you follow, the comment you try to post — so the ask arrives exactly when saying yes is worth something. And for the community itself, registration isn't enough: clinicians claim a verified profile — find your record, verify your identity, then join the discussion. Comments ship with the unglamorous completeness that keeps communities alive: report flows, moderation states, empty states, minimum-length nudges.

Tradeoff: anonymous-first delays the sign-up metric on purpose. The bet: an account created at a moment of value outlasts ten created at a gate.

05Experience Design

Designed for stolen minutes

The platform's restraint is deliberate. Notifications offer exactly two frequencies: once a day, or off — a news product that refuses to become noise. The header counts minutes saved today. Sessions end cleanly: load more if you want, leave if you're done. Even conversion respects the clock — nudges appear at value moments, never as interruptions to reading.

One platform, every gap

Briefs for the two-minute check, the dive-in for the ten-minute story, podcasts for the commute (Featured, Conferences, By Curie, Case Studies), games for the waiting room, the newsletter for the week's recap — the same clinician, met at whatever minute their day offers. Bookmarks and history keep every path resumable; desktop and mobile web carry the whole experience.

06Validation & Iteration

The boards keep their own record of the iteration:

  • Two home directions, each with its rationale written beside it — variety-first stacked tiles versus a focus-first single tile with swipe gestures — and the swipe version taken to a working prototype in iOS Safari, judged in the hand rather than from stills
  • Three placements explored for bookmarks and history — segmented control, floating pills, bottom-sheet toggle — before one won
  • The dive-in page rebuilt section by section, each change annotated with its reason: the audience-tabbed "Why it matters", the one-metric-at-a-time tabs, the in-place sources reveal

07Impact & Learnings

What changed in practice

What the design delivers is visible in the product's own shape: a clinician can arrive anonymous, clear the day's relevant news in minutes, go deep only where it matters, and leave — with the platform quietly counting the minutes it gave back.

Honest limits on what I can claim

ZoomRx work; specific performance data stays internal. This case study covers the design reasoning at the level I can share.

Learnings I've taken forward

When your user's scarcest resource is time, the product's job is to give some back — and to say so. Curie prints its value in its own header.

And personalisation worth having is assembled by the user in taps — a follow here, a specialty there — never demanded through a settings page.

01Executive Summary

Eighteen months ago I would not have written a line of production code. This site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript — no framework, no build step, no tracker, no template — carrying seven case studies, a theme system, in-page routing and a handful of interactions that had to feel designed rather than bolted on. I designed it, and I built it by directing Claude: describing intent, reviewing every iteration on a local server, rejecting what didn't work, and keeping only what a recruiter would actually benefit from.

The point isn't that a designer can code now. It's that the loop between deciding and shipping collapsed, and the judgement about what to ship — and what to leave out — became the whole job.

One file. HTML, CSS and JavaScript. One external dependency (fonts). Seven case studies inside it. The original September 2026 build went through four versions in one week.

02Problem & Opportunity

My portfolio lived in two places: a Framer landing page for the first impression and Notion for the case studies. Moving between products created an avoidable hand-off for readers. Framer could not hold long-form case studies well; Notion could not make a first impression. Neither was mine to control end to end.

The opportunity was newer than the problem. With an AI collaborator, a designer can now own the whole surface — the writing, the design, and the code that carries them — if the designer is willing to direct rather than delegate.

Design challenge

How does a designer ship a production-quality website alone, at the speed of a design iteration, without it becoming a monument to the tools that built it?

03Insights That Shaped the Product

The site is the seventh case study

Every visual and interaction on the site is evidence in front of the very people evaluating craft. That cut both ways: anything half-finished would be judged as harshly as a half-finished screen in a case study.

Usability for a recruiter beats novelty for a designer

The design assumes a recruiter comparing several portfolios under time pressure. Every effect had to answer "does this help them decide faster?" — a rule that killed as many ideas as it kept.

Directing AI is a design skill, not a technical one

The code was never the hard part. The hard part was knowing what to ask for, recognising what was wrong on screen, and holding the line on a decision through four rebuilds.

04Key Product Decisions

Decision 1 — No framework, one file

What we didn't do: a React or Next.js build, the default answer for anything "modern." It would have added a toolchain, a deploy pipeline and a dependency tree to a site that needed none of them.

Plain HTML, CSS and vanilla JavaScript in a single file. It keeps its core interface in one file, hosts anywhere, and can be read in full by anyone who presses View Source — which is the point of a portfolio.

Tradeoff: one file is easy to host and hard to diff; a change to one case study means opening the full HTML document. Accepted, because a portfolio changes rarely and gets read constantly.

Decision 2 — Notion stays the base; the site replicates it

Case studies were originally authored in Notion and converted into the site. This preview retains those narratives, with focused edits for current positioning and consistency. Future content updates need to be reconciled with the source.

Tradeoff: a generated HTML preview is easy to share, but source content and published copy must stay aligned.

Decision 3 — Case studies open in place

What we didn't do: seven separate pages with their own URLs, the conventional structure.

The seven case studies live inside the home page as views, with hash routing so deep links still work. Clicking a case opens it instantly; the recruiter never leaves.

Tradeoff: no per-case URLs for search engines. A portfolio is found through a person, not a search — accepted.

Decision 4 — Interactions that do a job

The theme switch is the O in PRODUCT. Skill stickers wiggle. Phone mockups float. Every one of these was kept when it supported the clarity and usability goals, and each respects reduced-motion settings.

What we didn't do: a bento-grid hero. We built it, looked at it, and pulled it back to a big-type hero and an open intro — the bento survived only for the proof section, where a grid earns its keep.
Tradeoff: restraint reads as less impressive on first glance than a wall of effects. It reads as more senior on the second.

05Experience Design

The original design iterations

The original build explored several visual directions before settling on the large-type hero, the theme toggle within PRODUCT, the skill stickers and the proof grid. This revision retains those choices and makes focused changes for clarity and navigation.

The recruiter layer

Two reading scenarios guide the structure: a recruiter scanning for fit and a design manager exploring the decisions. The “In 10 seconds” panel, “My role” line, defining-call marker and case-study section links support those paths. These are design scenarios, not measured recruiter research.

06Validation & Iteration

The original design was developed through iterative reviews. This refinement preserves that direction and focuses on navigation, readable copy, responsive layout rules and clear attribution. Browser and real-device visual checks remain the next validation step for this preview.

07Impact & Learnings

What changed in practice

One surface instead of two. A site that keeps its core interface in one file, works on a phone, opens any case study instantly, and can be read in source by any engineer on the hiring panel. And a working demonstration — in front of the evaluator — of the skill the case studies describe: deciding what to build, and directing an AI to build exactly that.

Honest limits on what I can claim

This is a portfolio, not a product with users; there are no engagement metrics to report. I didn't hand-write the code — I directed it, reviewed it and rejected it until it was right, and I'd rather say so than let anyone assume otherwise.

Learnings I've taken forward

The bottleneck moved. When shipping is nearly free, taste and restraint are the scarce resources — and what we didn't do becomes the most important line in every decision. That was true of Sagan, Gribb and Bentley. It turned out to be true of this page.
